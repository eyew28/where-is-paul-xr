Wild Teenage Years in Sofia

1994–2004

Early Days in the Capital

Moving to Sofia at the age of six marked the beginning of what would become a formative chapter in my life. The transition from the coastal vibes of Varna to the bustling energy of Bulgaria's capital city was both exciting and challenging. We landed first in Mladost — Youth, the name they gave the first big prefab neighborhood, panelki lined up toward Vitosha. About a year there. Then we moved west, to Lyulin 2, and that is where the rest of Sofia happened.

Lyulin 2

Lyulin is not downtown. It is the largest housing complex in the country — a city inside the city, ten microdistricts of panel blocks, the ones in Lyulin 2 numbered in the 200s. Built from the '70s into the mid-'90s, mostly Bs-69 series concrete. Trolleybuses, later the metro. Between the blocks: the courtyards, the shops, the concrete that doubled as a soccer pitch. This was home. The remainder of my time in Sofia was here.

90th School — José de San Martín

The school was in the neighborhood: 90. СУ „Ген. Хосе де Сан Мартин“, on what was then ulitsa 204 in Lyulin 2. Founded 1976, still named after a communist when the complex went up, then in 1993 — the year before I arrived in Sofia — they gave it José de San Martín, the Argentine general. That is the name on the building I walked to. High school, down the street from the block.

I started Sofia school life at 119th, grades 1–6, then the German years at Doris Tenedi for 7–10. José de San Martín is the Lyulin school — the one that belonged to the microdistrict, the one you didn't commute across the city to reach.

Adventures in the City

By the time I turned 13, I was already exploring the vibrant nightlife of Sofia. The city's downtown area became our playground, where my friends and I would hang out, discovering the pulse of urban life. We weren't just typical teenagers – we were young explorers, eager to experience everything the city had to offer.

Street Soccer and Scars

One of my most vivid memories from those years is playing street soccer on the concrete between the Lyulin blocks. We didn't need fancy fields or proper equipment – just a ball and the kids from the neighborhood. Those intense matches left their mark, quite literally – the scars on my knees still tell stories of spectacular dives and passionate plays. Each scar is a reminder of the carefree days when we played until the street lights came on.

Shaping the Future

Looking back, those years in Sofia were more than just a period of education and play. They were the foundation of my adventurous spirit and my ability to adapt to new environments. The combination of formal education at two very different schools, the freedom to explore the city, and the friendships formed during those years all contributed to shaping the person I would become.

The streets of Sofia, with their mix of history and modernity, became my classroom beyond the school walls. Every corner held a new adventure, every friend a new perspective, and every experience a lesson in life. These early years in Sofia weren't just about growing up – they were about growing into myself.

Sofia in the '90s: the air we actually breathed

The Sofia I ran around in was not the Sofia of malls and a metro map. It was the decade after the lid came off. The Dimitrov mausoleum still squatted in the center of town, empty, used as a toilet and a rally stage, until they blew it up in the summer of 1999. Second-hand Opels were replacing Ladas. A handful of people got suddenly, cartoonishly rich. Everyone else was counting lev against the Deutsche mark. The winter of 1996–97, when I was eight, the currency fell through the floor — seventy to the dollar in autumn, past three thousand by New Year. Students froze in the streets around fires. BNT, which had ignored the protests, started playing the Beatles' Let It Be around the news. I was at 119th High School, learning city life. The city was learning capitalism in public.

What I actually grew up on: MTV, B.T.R., Friends, and Dallas. And Kiss — because of my brother.

MTV was the portal. That fractured neon logo on a set you had to hit on the side, a pirate dish, a friend who knew a guy. It was not a channel. It was proof that the rest of the world had sound. On the regular TV, Dallas was the American oil-and-shoulder-pads epic the whole country had already decided to take personally. And Friends — six people on a couch in a city I had never seen — was the other classroom. I learned English from school. I learned how Americans talked to each other from that apartment.

B.T.R.

B.T.R. was ours — Bulgarian rock that didn't wait for permission, the band you claimed if you wanted guitars instead of whatever was leaking out of the kiosks. Sofia band. Our band. I still hear them when I think about those years.

Kiss, because of my brother

My brother put Kiss in my hands. He was a big fan. Makeup, the riffs, the whole cartoon-of-rock thing that is also deadly serious when you are a kid in Sofia and this is what America sounded like. I still listen to them. That never wore off.
