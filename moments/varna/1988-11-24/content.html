Growing Up in Varna

1988–1994

I was born in Varna on the 24th of November, 1988. The Black Sea was already cold. The Sea Garden was already the city's living room — that long green strip between the apartment blocks and the water, where Varna has been taking its evening walk for a hundred years. I spent my first years on those beaches. That's the whole memory, and also the whole point: sand, salt, Bulgarian summer, the sound of a city that still called itself the Sea Capital of a country that was about to change its name.

The year the old world ended

I don't remember the night it happened. I was eleven months old on November 10, 1989, when state TV told Bulgaria that Todor Zhivkov — thirty-five years in power, the Kremlin's most loyal man in the Balkans — had been "relieved of his duties." The Berlin Wall had come down the evening before. In Varna the news arrived on the First Program, the way all news arrived: one channel, one voice, a Yunost set in the kitchen if you still had black-and-white. People who were old enough to understand it still talk about that broadcast the way other people talk about where they were when a president was shot.

What I inherited instead of the memory was the weather after the storm. Empty shelves. Lines. Oranges and bananas that showed up once a year, if they showed up. Christmas was not a holiday the state recognized. Kindergarten still had comrades for teachers, until one day it didn't, and the word "miss" appeared like a new color. Pink, people say, arrived with the Wall. So did the word "supermarket," which for a while meant a rumor more than a building.

A resort city pretending to be a port

Varna itself was never just politics. It was a seaside town with imperial ruins, socialist monuments, and a beach that didn't care which flag was on the post office. Roman baths under the downtown streets — Odessos, third-largest thermae in the Empire. A cathedral that looks like it was dropped onto the Black Sea from Moscow. A dolphinarium from 1984 that every Bulgarian kid of my generation can still smell. The Sea Garden, laid out by a Czech gardener who had trained at Schönbrunn, with an Alley of Cosmonauts where Gagarin planted a silver fir in 1961.

What was on in the house

Two programs, both part-time. Kids' films in the afternoon. Every Sunday — Всяка Неделя — which the whole country treated like church. And then the American shows the house would not miss: Dallas above all, oil money and family war dubbed into Bulgarian, the West as a soap. MTV was not in the crib with me. MTV was a rumor from people who had a cousin with a satellite dish. That rumor would become the whole soundtrack later, in Sofia, with B.T.R. on the tapes and Friends on the set. Here it was still the sea, the garden, and whatever the First Program decided we were allowed to watch.
