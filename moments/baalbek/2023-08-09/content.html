Baalbek — The Stones That Shouldn't Exist

August 9, 2023

A day in the Bekaa, inland from Beirut, standing inside the Temple of Bacchus. That is the kind of ruin that makes you understand why people used to think gods needed architecture. Baalbek is not a postcard stop. It is one of the largest Roman sanctuaries ever built, sitting at 1,150 meters on the edge of a farm valley, and it is still, somehow, not famous enough.

What this place was

People have been on this hill since the Bronze Age. Ceramics from the fourth and third millennia BCE turned up under the Great Court. The name is older than Rome: Baalbek, city of Baal — the storm and fertility god Hadad, worshiped here long before anyone spoke Latin. The Phoenicians ran a triad. Rome did what Rome always did: they kept the cult and changed the nametags.

Alexander's people called it Heliopolis, Sun City. Under Rome it became a pilgrimage machine — Jupiter Optimus Maximus Heliopolitanus, Venus, Mercury. Pilgrims came from Athens, Rome, Gaul, even near Hadrian's Wall. The god here was an oracle. Macrobius says they put the statue in a litter and the bearers, after fasting and shaving their heads, felt which way to walk. Trajan was told he would not come back from Parthia. He didn't.

Temple of Jupiter

The Temple of Jupiter is the giant. Fifty-four Corinthian columns, each about 20 meters high — the tallest of the classical world. Ten across the front, nineteen down the sides. Construction probably started in the first century, maybe under Augustus; a graffito dated AD 60 says they were already near the top of a column drum. It took more than two centuries to assemble the whole sanctuary. Earthquakes, Christian emperors, and Justinian picking columns for Hagia Sophia in Constantinople did the rest. Six columns still stand on the south side, with their entablature, 19 meters in the air. You stand next to one and the scale stops being a number.

The Trilithon

Under the Jupiter podium, in the west retaining wall, sit three limestone blocks called the Trilithon. Each is about 19 meters long, 4 meters high, 3.6 meters thick, roughly 750–800 tons. Below them, another course of 350-ton stones. These are among the largest building blocks ever set into a wall by anyone. The quarry was slightly uphill, so they did not have to lift the monsters — they could roll them on banks of earth. That is the boring, correct, still-insane explanation. Local stories prefer jinn, or giants working for Nimrod after the Flood. You look at the seam and both versions feel equally plausible for a second.

Temple of Bacchus

South of Jupiter, lower, better preserved, is the temple I actually stood in. They call it Bacchus. There is no inscription that proves the name; the decoration is Bacchic, the date is late second century, Antonine. It is smaller than Jupiter and still larger than the Parthenon. Eight by fifteen unfluted Corinthian columns. A staircase with three flights. Inside: two rows of niches that once held statues, a raised holy of holies you were not supposed to look into, a crypt for the cult vessels. The stone ceiling of the peristyle is still mostly there — gods' busts in hexagons and lozenges. Earthquakes, a medieval dungeon, a change of religions, and it is still standing. That is the miracle, not the name.

The stone they never moved

Nine hundred meters from the temples, still in the quarry, lies Hajjar el-Hibla — the Stone of the Pregnant Woman. About 1,000 tons, never fully cut free of the bedrock. Two even larger blocks were found later in the same pit: the Stone of the South around 1,240 tons, and the Forgotten Stone, found in 2014, around 1,500–1,650 tons. They were probably meant for the Jupiter podium and never left. One story for the name: a pregnant woman said she knew how to move it if they fed her until she gave birth. Another: touch it and you get pregnant. Another: jinn. The block does not care. It is still there.

After Rome

Christians built a basilica in the Great Court. Some of them decided Solomon must have built the place, because pagans could not have. Lightning hit it in the 520s — irony, if you remember Jupiter was a storm god. The Arabs took the city in 637 and the old name came back: Baalbek. The Umayyad mosque on the former forum is one of the oldest in Islam. Crusaders and Mamluks turned the sanctuary into a fort — people still call it the Qalaa. Suleiman pulled columns for a mosque in Istanbul. A 1759 earthquake dropped the Bacchus lintel. Kaiser Wilhelm II showed up in 1898 and sent archaeologists; his name is cut into a Bacchus wall, which is a very emperor thing to do.

UNESCO listed it in 1984. It is still the finest Imperial Roman architecture at full volume, fused with a Phoenician cult that never really left. Venus has a round temple of her own, almost delicate next to the giants. Mercury's temple is mostly a rock-cut stair on Sheikh Abdallah hill.

One day

One day is not enough and also exactly enough. You go. You stand in Bacchus. You look at six columns that used to be fifty-four. You leave smaller. The Bekaa keeps growing food around it like the stones are just another crop that didn't come up.
